# Chapter 5 — Translating Jargon to Plain English

Research papers speak in compressed packages: "double-blind randomized placebo-
controlled parallel-group trial" *is* an information-dense sentence, but it feels
locked. In this chapter you'll learn that the code is mostly made of small, load-
bearing words — and that you already have the instincts to break them apart.

## The 3-step translation method

Whenever you hit a wall of unfamiliar terms:

### Step 1: Break the word into its known parts

Researchers build jargon like LEGO. Most of it is made of common English bricks:

- "randomized" → people were assigned by chance, like shuffling a deck
- "controlled" → someone got a comparison treatment to measure against
- "blind" / "double-blind" → nobody knew who got what (participants;
  double = the measurers didn't either)
- "prospective" → the study ran forward in time (planned, then done)
- "retrospective" → the authors looked backward at records
- "cohort" → a group of people followed over time
- "meta-analysis" → a study of studies, pooled together
- "in vitro" → in glass — cells in a dish
- "in vivo" → in living bodies
- "pilot" → small first-draft study
- "benchmark" → a scoring test to compare against

Splitting a phrase down to its bricks turns "double-blind placebo-controlled
randomized trial" into: *"a real experiment where nobody knew who got the real thing
vs. the fake pill, and chance decided."* You do it all the time in other areas of
life — you already know how to break unfamiliar language down.

### Step 2: Trust the paper to define its own terms

Most journals require unfamiliar terms to be defined **at first use** — usually in
the abstract or the intro's first mention, sometimes in a paper-glossary. If you
met a term at the bottom of page 6, its definition was likely on page 2 and you
pasted by it without recognizing it. Ctrl+F the abstract for the same word: nine
times out of ten, the plain version was right there three paragraphs before you
needed it.

Failing that, the abbreviation list right at the paper's front or back ("PDF-version
abbreviations") is usually the cheapest dictionary in the whole document.

### Step 3: Build your own glossary as you read

Keep a "words I had to look up" note while you read any paper. After four or five
papers from one field, you'll notice something delightful: the vocabulary feels big
but is actually *tiny* — the same 30–60 words do most of the work everywhere. Your
personal cheat-sheet becomes more useful than any textbook; it's in your words, at
your speed.

## A starter translation table

Copy this into your own notes and grow it. Plain-English translations are earnest,
not perfect — that's the spirit:

| Paper word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| Abstract | the paper's 30-second trailer |
| Methods | the recipe |
| Results | what the recipe produced |
| Discussion | the authors' sales pitch and honest hesitation combined |
| Limitations | the authors' own list of what could be wrong |
| n | number of participants (people, mice, cells) tested |
| Endpoint / outcome | the specific thing they measured to answer their question |
| Primary outcome | the *planned* main measure, decided up front |
| Hazard ratio / odds ratio ≈ 1.0 | no difference found (numbers > or < 1 point to direction) |
| CI (confidence interval) | a likely range for the true answer — the narrower, the surer |
| p < 0.05 | "small chance this pattern is luck" — check, not proof |
| Statistically significant | unlikely to be luck — says nothing about how big the effect is |
| Placebo | fake treatment; measures how much just *expecting* help does |
| Standard of care | the normal treatment patients currently get |
| Contraindicated | unsafe to combine (this drug + that condition) |
| Morbidity / mortality | sickness rate / death rate |
| Confidence interval overlapping zero | result is not different from no effect |
| Retrospective | authors looked backward at existing records |
| Cross-sectional | a snapshot of one moment in time |
| Longitudinal | the same people followed over time |
| Independent variable | the thing they changed |
| Dependent variable | the thing they measured |

## Using AI as a translation helper (with a safety railing)

This is where you can pull in the AI tools covered elsewhere
(ai-for-nurse-educators-guide, patient-teaching-with-ai-guide). Used carefully, an
AI chatbot is a patient, always-available friend for jargon. Here's the railing that
keeps it safe:

1. **Ask it for the plain-English reading** of a tricky paragraph — "Explain this
   sentence like a friend would, no fancy words" is a completely fair prompt.
2. **Then ask "which words in my sentence stand for which words in yours?"** —
   forces the AI to keep its answer tied to the paper's language, instead of drifting
   into a friendly essay.
3. **Feed the AI the paragraph itself**, not a summary of it. It can only be faithful
   to what it can see, and papers are copyright material — paste a paragraph for
   *your own study use*, not for republishing.
4. **Verify anything that will matter.** AI can be confidently wrong — especially
   with numbers, names, and doses. For anything that feeds your verdict, check the
   AI's claim against the paper itself, or a second source (a lecture PDF, a
   textbook, your glossary table from an earlier chapter).
5. **Never use it to skip.* The trick is to use AI to *get you back to the paper
   faster*, not to replace reading it. Read the original sentence after every AI
   translation — noticing what changed is where you get good fast.

## The magic sentence pattern

Research writing actually has plain-English bones. The most common sentences in
every paper shape into this pattern:

> **"We asked [Q]. To find out, we did [D]. We found [F]. It could mean [I]."**

Practical move: take any paragraph that defeats you, and rewrite it yourself into
that four-part sentence. You're allowed to write clumsy versions — clumsy in your
own words beats fluent in someone else's. This also plants a flag you can compare
to the Discussion: did you understand it the same way the authors did?

> **Education only.** Translation makes papers readable; it doesn't turn papers into
> advice. If your plain-English takeaway inspires a decision about your health or
> someone else's, give it to a professional to translate again — they'll see things
> you're not trained to see, and that's their superpower, not your failure.
