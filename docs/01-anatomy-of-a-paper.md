# Chapter 1 — Anatomy of a Paywalled PDF

You finally found the paper. You click. A paywall. Then — either through a library, a
friend, a preprint, or the author's own website — you get the PDF. And now you're
staring at six columns of small type and wondering where to even begin.

This chapter walks through a paper the way a builder walks through a house: room by
room, with a purpose for each one. Once you know what each part is *for*, you can
decide which parts to read closely and which to skip guilt-free.

## Where things live on a typical paper

Most papers in medicine, science, and social research follow the same floor plan.
Here's the map, in the order you'd meet it:

### 1. Title, authors, and affiliations

The title is often the single most informative sentence in the paper — more honest
than the abstract, even. Note the *shape* of it:

- "**Randomized controlled trial of...**" tells you the gold-standard design was used.
- "**Association between...**" tells you these are linked observations, not a
  cause-and-effect experiment.
- "**A case study / proof of concept / pilot study**" tells you you're looking at a
  first small step, and that's fine — just set the bar accordingly.

Author lines show names plus affiliations, like "Department of Nutrition, State
University." Worth a five-second scan: if every author works for a company selling
the tested product, that's a fact you'll want in your verdict (Chapter 4).

### 2. The abstract

A 150–300 word summary of the whole paper: question, method, result numbers, and the
authors' bottom line. It is genuinely useful and genuinely a trap — *both at the same
time.*

Useful, because it tells you fast whether the paper deserves your time. A trap,
because abstracts are the most polished writing in the paper, written to sell the
finding, and they sometimes promise more than the paper delivers. Rule of thumb: the
abstract decides *whether you read on* — never *what you believe*.

### 3. Introduction

A few paragraphs of backstory: what was already known, what was still unclear, and
therefore what question this paper set out to answer. Beginner readers skip this. That's
usually a mistake — the introduction is where the authors tell you *the question*,
in the same sentence pattern almost every time: *"...however, it remains unknown
whether X."* That sentence is the paper's job description.

### 4. Methods

The recipe. Who was studied, how many, how they were picked, what was measured, and
what comparisons were made. This section is technical by design — it's how other
researchers check the work was done honestly.

You don't need to understand every word (Chapter 5 helps with the words), but you do
need three specific facts from here for your verdict:

- **Who and how many** (n = 40? n = 4,000? humans or mice?)
- **How people got into the study** (volunteers? everyone at certain clinics? randomly assigned?)
- **What was measured, and when** (six weeks? one lab reading? a self-reported diary?)

### 5. Results

The findings, presented as text plus tables and figures. Key habit: an honest paper's
numbers live here, and the discussion should match them. When you reach the
discussion, you can play a matching game — for each bold claim, ask *which number in
Results backs this up?* If you can't find it, that's a finding.

Some papers label results sections by outcome, e.g. "Primary outcome" and
"Secondary outcomes." Primary is what the study was built to test — the secondary
results are bonuses, and people sometimes treat bonuses as headlines.

### 6. Discussion

Where the authors tell you what they think it all means. Three beats, almost always
in this order:

1. **They restate their finding** — in the authors' favor.
2. **Limitations** — they describe what could be wrong, starting with a sentence like
   "Our study has several limitations." Do not skip this. Seriously: this list is the
   most honest paragraph in the paper, written by its own critics (the authors).
3. **What's next** — "future studies should..." which tells you the field itself
   considers this preliminary.

### 7. References

The bibliography. You don't read it first — but it tells you whether this paper is
building on well-known work or on thin air. Friendly trick: find one or two
"foundational" references that several papers in the field cite, and read *those*
first instead. Sometimes an older, clearer paper is a better place to start.

### 8. Things people miss (the fine print worth reading)

- **Funding statement** — who paid. Appears after the references or on the first page,
  e.g. "This work was supported by........"  (Chapter 4.)
- **Conflict-of-interest statement** — what the authors' employers and payments are.
- **Trial registration number** — for human trials, something like
  "Registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT12345678." Registration happens *before* the
  trial runs, and it's how you check the team didn't quietly change the rules.
- **Acknowledgments** — often includes stats help, writing help, and reconsideration
  about who really wrote the paper.
- **Supplementary materials** — an appendix with extra tables. It can be a light
  lesson: the main paper shows the pretty summary, and the supplements show
  everything else the researchers measured.

## Getting the PDF in the first place (the legal, friendly way)

Paywalls are real and frustrating. Here are routes that cost nothing and are on the
right side of the rules:

1. **Your public library.** Many library systems and university alumni programs
   provide access to journals or interlibrary loan, where the library requests a copy
   from another library for you. Ask at the desk — this is a service they love to
   provide, and it's shockingly underused.
2. **PubMed Central (PMC).** For biomedical papers, `pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` shows
   an abstract, and many papers have a full free copy marked "Free article" or "PMC."
   Federal-funder rules require many of these to be deposited there.
3. **Preprints.** Many authors post a free, pre-peer-review copy on medRxiv, bioRxiv,
   or before the version. It is *not* identical to the published version — treat it
   as "the author's draft" — but it's often 100% readable and free.
4. **The author's own site.** Search the first author's name plus a title word.
   Academics often legally host their own PDFs.
5. **Emailing the corresponding author.** The weirdest advice in this chapter, and it
   works: authors are generally delighted when a non-academic wants to read their
   paper. One polite email gets you the PDF much more often than you'd expect.
6. **Open-access journals.** Some journals make everything free by design. If the
   publisher's page shows "Open Access" you get it with no account.

And one quiet discourage-and-move-on: skip file-sharing sites that offer free copies
they aren't entitled to share. They're illegal, they sometimes host malware, and the
legal options above cover you better.

## The two-minute version of this chapter

Before any close reading, spend two minutes answering:

- What is the *question* (introduction's last sentence)?
- What *kind* of study is it (title and methods)?
- Who paid and who worked where (funding and affiliation lines)?
- What are the *limitations the authors admit* (Discussion section)?

You've now got the scaffolding — the four-pass method in [Chapter 2](02-four-pass-method.md)
tells you what to do with it.

> **Education only.** This chapter teaches reading habits, not how to judge medical
> decisions. That's what your care team is for — bring them your verdict and let them
> place it in context.
