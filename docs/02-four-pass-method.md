# Chapter 2 — The Four-Pass Reading Method

Here's the secret about reading research papers: **you're not supposed to read them
front to back, once, carefully.** Even researchers don't. Papers are dense because
they're written for people who already live inside a field's vocabulary. You read
them in *passes* — each pass with a different, small job — and each pass gets easier
than brute-force reading ever was.

This method adapts S. Keshav's famous "three-pass" system from computer science into
a fourth pass tuned for non-specialists, since our extra pass is where you drill down
on trust: sample sizes, funding, and limitations.

## Pass 1 — The Skim (5 minutes)

**Job: decide whether this paper deserves the next 45 minutes of your life.**

Read only:

- Title and abstract
- Every section heading (they're the paper's table of contents in disguise)
- The last two paragraphs of the Discussion (the authors' own summary)
- Glance at every figure and table *caption* — captions are written to be read
  standalone, so they're beginner-friendly by design.

Ask: *Is this actually about my question? What kind of study is it? How big?* Now
decide: keep it or drop it. Dropping a paper is a legitimate, Skilled Reader move —
you just saved an hour.

## Pass 2 — The Map (15–20 minutes)

**Job: build a mental map of the paper's argument, without drowning in detail.**

Read the Introduction and Discussion fully this time. For each section after that,
read only the first and last paragraph. At the top of your page, finish these two
sentences:

- **The question was:** __________
- **The answer the authors say they got:** __________

Also start a "words I don't know" list — write them down, don't stop and linger;
Pass 4 (and [Chapter 5](05-jargon-to-plain-english.md)) handles them. Finally,
skim the figures like captions' larger siblings: what does the pattern *look* like?

The Map pass leaves you able to explain the paper to a friend in three sentences —
even though you haven't understood its statistics at all. That's normal, and it's
further than most people ever get.

## Pass 3 — The Read (30–60 minutes)

**Job: earn the claim. Read Methods and Results carefully, and connect them back to
the Discussion.**

Now the careful work, in this order:

1. **Methods first.** Who was in the study, how they got there, what was compared,
   what was measured. Your verdict's "who and how many" fields get filled here.
2. **Results second.** Read with the Discussion's claims in hand. For each major
   claim, find its supporting number or figure. This matching game is the most
   powerful credibility check in this whole guide: no matching number = a claim
   running ahead of its data.
3. **Figures third.** Read the axes. Look for the y-axis scale trick (Chapter 3).
4. **The limitations list.** Copy the authors' admitted limitations into your notes.
   These aren't weaknesses in the paper; they're the authors doing your critique for
   you.

Beginner trap: thinking you should understand every abbreviation and every statistical
test by now. Don't. Save them for Pass 4.

## Pass 4 — The Verdict (10 minutes)

**Job: turn your reading into a decision about what to do with the paper.**

Fill out the [One-Page Verdict Template](verdict-template.md). At minimum:

- **Who was studied and how many** — from Methods.
- **The main result in absolute numbers** — not just "improved!" but "5 in 100 went
  to 3 in 100."
- **Who funded it, and any conflicts** — Chapter 4.
- **Your confidence**: high / medium / low — with one sentence of *why*.
- **A plain-English takeaway**, spoken style: "If a friend asked me what this paper
  says, I'd tell them…"
- **What you'll do**: nothing yet / watch for more research / worth acting on /
  ask a professional about it.

Pass 4 is also the pass where you tackle your jargon list, look up two or three of
the cited references if the paper leans hard on them, and decide where this paper
sits among everything else you've read on the topic.

## Why passes work (the one-sentence theory)

Each pass gives your brain a *specific small job* with a visible finish line, which
is why this method is kinder to tired brains than "read it all carefully" ever was.
You're never lost in the middle of a paper again — you always know which pass you're
in and what its finish looks like.

## A gentle pacing note

A "hard" paper is a paper read too fast. Five passes at 10 minutes each beats one
grunt sweep, every time. Give yourself permission to put a paper down between passes
and come back — the map you built in Pass 2 will still be there when you return.

> **Education only.** The verdict you build is your reading compass, not a medical
> or legal conclusion. Share it with a professional before acting on anything that
> touches health, law, or money.
