# Chapter 4 — Sample Sizes and Conflicts of Interest

Two quiet questions flip almost every paper from a monologue into an accountability
conversation: **who was studied, and who paid?** Neither question requires any
statistics background. Both are printed right there in the paper. Most readers just
never look.

## Sample size — what "n" actually tells you

Papers constantly write things like "n = 1,204." The n just means *number of study
participants* (or mice, or cells). You don't need formulas to use it — a few
intuitions carry you very far.

### Intuition 1: bigger buckets, steadier water

Thirty people is a good start; three thousand is a lot harder to be wrong about. Tiny
studies aren't worthless — early, foundational work is often small — but a life-changing
conclusion built on thirty people is like hearing one perfect note from an orchestra
and judging the whole symphony.

### Intuition 2: small studies are noisier

Flip a coin ten times and you'll frequently get seven heads. Flip it ten thousand
times and you'll almost never wander far from five heads out of ten. Small samples
wander. So when you read " Supplement group improved 15% (n = 22)," a fair question
is: *would that improvement repeat with 220 more people, or was it the coin-wander?*
You don't need to compute that — you just need to know the question exists, and that
small findings stay "suggestive" until big studies repeat them.

### Intuition 3: who the people *are* matters as much as how many

n = 5,000 is unimpressive if all 5,000 are healthy 22-year-old university students,
and you're wondering about your 68-year-old father. Bigger isn't automatically better;
**bigger-in-the-right-people** is. From the Methods section, ask three: who got in,
who got left out, and does that population resemble the person you're reading about?

### Intuition 4: statistical significance is not importance

When a paper says a result was "statistically significant," it means: *this pattern
would be unusual if there were truly nothing going on.* It does **not** mean the
effect is big, important, or useful. A study of 100,000 people can find a tiny,
meaningless-but-significant difference; a study of 40 people can contain something
big but not significant. Read the effect *size* (how big was the change?) and
significance (is it likely real?) as two different dials.

Bonus intuition, worth remembering forever: with a large enough group, *trivially
tiny* differences become "significant." Significance without size is a receipt for
a purchase that doesn't fill the bag.

### A worked example

- Study A: n = 38, improvement from 10 points to 12 points, "not statistically significant."
- Study B: n = 9,000 people, improvement from 10 points to 10.4 points,
  "statistically significant."

Study B is doing *better science*, and its finding might be completely unimportant
for any single person. Study A is *smaller* and its finding might be the one that
matters — it just hasn't been proven yet. Both need a verdict, not a knee-jerk.
Now when you see "sea of n's," read them like fishing reports: what size pond, what
kind of fish, and how big was the catch, really?

## Conflicts of interest — who paid, and why it matters

A "conflict of interest" (COI) is any situation where the people doing or publishing
the study have an interest in it coming out a certain way — money, jobs, patents,
careers, hearts. Disclosure isn't a scandal; undisclosed influence is.

### Where COI information hides

- **Funding statement** — usually unglamorous text after the reference list:
  "This work received grant NNN from X."
- **Author affiliations** — the departmental addresses on page 1. If the
  nutritional-supplement study was done by "Head of Research, Acme Supplements
  Ltd.," that's a loop with known shape.
- **Conflict-of-interest statement** — "Dr. Y reports consulting fees from Z."
  Many journals now require this; missing ones are worth noticing as a fact.
- **Acknowledgments + study steering committees** — sometimes more money names
  appear here than anywhere else.
- **Trial registries** — ClinicalTrials.gov entries let you find who asked for the
  trial in the first place.

### What to do with it (realistically)

Disclosure does not turn a paper into a lie, and burdening yourself with paranoia
would be counterproductive. Conflicts are context, and they sharpen questions:

- Money doesn't prevent honest work; it *tilts*. Universally funded and industry
  funded studies can both be flawed, and both can be great.
- Strong findings that would still be strong *with* the tiniest tilt are worth more
  than thin findings that would flip with a slight shove.
- The best question is one of replication: has *anyone without this funding*
  reproduced the result? Until then, store the finding with a sticky note.
- Author careers count. "Publish or perish" is a real pressure, and the pressure is
  on doctors, professors, companies, and all authors. Positive findings get
  published more easily than negative ones — the whole literature breathes that air.

### Disappearing results — the sad habit worth knowing

Nobody publishes a paper whose pill doesn't work. So the mix of studies in a field
isn't a random sample; it's a library with lots of success stories and vanishingly
few failures, sometimes called **publication bias**. Meta-analyses try to estimate
and correct for this. You can too: if you've read ten small, positive studies and
you can't find one negative one, that silence is itself information.

## Two-minute accountability check

At the end of Pass 1, look only at page one and the end pages, and write down:

- **Who and how many?** (n, species, who they resemble)
- **Who funded? Who works where? Any COI statement?**
- **Was it registered?**

That's not cynicism. It's knowing who's buying the meal, how many diners tasted it,
and whether anyone else has tried the recipe. Chapter 3 helps you judge the taste.

> **Education only.** Sample-size and COI literacy make you a sharper reader, not a
> regulatory body. Take the verdict, not the voltage — health decisions belong with
> your care team, financial ones with your own judgment, and legal ones with
> professionals.
