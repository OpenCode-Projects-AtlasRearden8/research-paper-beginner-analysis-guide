# Chapter 3 — Strong Claims vs. Weak Claims

Research papers speak in a code of careful hedges, and once you can hear the code,
the paper tells you *itself* how confident to be: a claim standing on solid legs,
or a claim standing on tiptoes.

The fastest rule: **strong evidence says what happened; weak evidence says what
might, could, may, or is associated with.**

## The word wall: hedges that mean "thin"

Writers inside research are trained never to overclaim, so weak findings get
hedging language like a bubble-wrapped parcel. Look for these specific words:

| Word or phrase | What it usually signals |
|---|---|
| "may," "might," "could" | effect not established; hypothesis |
| "suggests," "suggestive of" | plausible, not demonstrated |
| "is associated with" | correlation; cause not shown |
| "linked to" | same as above |
| "hypothesis-generating" | author says: this is a first spark |
| "needs further study" | effect small or inconsistent so far |
| "in mice," "in vitro," "pilot" | not yet in humans, or not yet at scale |
| "novel," "first report" | no replication has ever happened |
| "promising" | marketing-adjacent word for "early" |

None of these mean the paper is wrong. They mean the paper is *early*. Your job is
to match your confidence to the stage, not to the marketing.

## Words that signal sturdier ground

These are the phrases you'll find when the evidence under a claim has more smarts:

- **"Randomized (controlled) trial" / "RCT"** — people were assigned to groups by
  chance, which defeats a huge family of bias tricks.
- **"Double-blind," "placebo-controlled"** — neither participants nor measurers knew
  who got what.
- **"Pre-registered" / "registered at ClinicalTrials.gov"** — the plan was written
  down *before* the data, so no after-the-fact goalpost moving.
- **"Replicated," "confirmed in," "consistent with previous findings"** — the same
  answer showed up more than once in different hands.
- **"Meta-analysis of N trials"** — a formal summary of many studies; its confidence
  depends on it honestly combining like-with-like (read the "inclusion criteria"
  carefully), and it can still be wrong — but it's usually the sturdiest floor in
  the house.
- **Absolute numbers given** — "reduced from 5% to 3%" is stronger writing than
  "reduced risk by 40%"; the second is the same fact made louder (see below).

## The two classic ways a true number becomes a misleading claim

Even honest papers can let a number walk into the discussion wearing a bigger
jacket. Catch these two reliably:

### 1. Relative risk dressed as absolute risk

"One in 10,000 people had this reaction; in the treatment group it was 2 in 10,000."
The relative change is "risk doubled!" — twice as likely! — and the absolute change
is one extra person in ten thousand. Both sentences describe the same data. Your
defense is a single habit: **always ask for the absolute numbers.** Take any
percentage-improvement claim and ask, "Out of how many people?"

### 2. Correlation dressed as cause

"People who drink more coffee get better scores on the memory test." Two readings,
and the paper can't choose between them from this kind of study: (a) coffee helps
memory, or (b) something about people who drink coffee — income, health habits,
&c. — also helps memory. Experimental studies (where researchers *assign* the
treatment) can separate those; observational studies (where researchers only
*watch*) can't. When the design is "we watched" and the conclusion is "therefore,"
gently mark the claim down a notch. The fix is easy when you read: keep the
exciting finding, subtract the "therefore."

## Check the matches: claim ↔ data

One of the strongest habits you can build: **read the Discussion with the Results
table open next to it.** For each claim sentence, ask:

- Which number is this resting on? Can I find it?
- Is the number from the *primary* outcome, or a secondary one being sold as the star?
- Does the claim say more than the number — for example, the study measured a
  biomarker and the discussion now talks about "living longer"?
- Is the claim about *further* work ("suggests a target for future therapy") wearing
  a *current-benefit* costume ("a new treatment")?

A mismatch isn't necessarily a scandal — some of it is normal enthusiasm — but every
mismatch is a claim to mark down in confidence.

## A tiny worked example

Methods: n = 30 volunteers, two weeks, measured by a questionnaire.
Abstract: "Our supplement may support healthy focus."

Hearing the code: "may" (hypothesis stage) + n = 30 (small) + self-report (subjective)
+ two weeks a short bridge to any long-term claim. The claim isn't false — it's a
*seed*, planted in a very small pot. Strong claim version of the same data would
need: hundreds of people, an objective measure, longer, randomized with a control
group, and probably an independent lab or two repeating it.

Now when the same finding arrives with a headline — "SCIENTISTS SAY THIS VITAMIN
CHANGES EVERYTHING" — you can do the quiet thing: compare the paper to the headline,
and smile. The paper said "may." The headline said "changes everything." Headlines
are written by people who never read past the abstract; you did.

## One-minute claim audit

For any claim, ask four questions:

1. **What design?** (watched vs. assigned; how many; how long; humans or mice)
2. **What words?** (hedged language or confirmed language)
3. **What numbers?** (absolute numbers findable in Results?)
4. **What would a fair skeptic say?** (limitations list, plus your own)

Answer those and you've done job of a peer reviewer's thinnest slice. Not bad for a
non-student.

> **Education only.** Claim-reading is a literacy skill, not a regulatory process.
> Don't use your claim audit to overrule a professional — use it to have better
> questions ready for the next conversation with one.
