# Glossary

The vocabulary of paper-reading, explained plainly. Words you'll meet in papers, in
journal websites, and in the other chapters of this guide.

## The document itself

- **Abstract** — the 150–300 word summary at the top of every paper. Use it to
  decide whether to read on; never as your final answer.
- **DOI** (Digital Object Identifier) — a paper's permanent address, like
  `doi:10.1234/abcd.1234`. Paste it anywhere to find the paper again.
- **Preprint** — a free copy an author posts before (or alongside) journal
  publication. Usually on medRxiv, bioRxiv, or arXiv. Readable and legal, but not
  yet peer reviewed — treat as an author's draft.
- **Peer review** — experts appointed by a journal who read a paper before
  publication and try to poke holes. A quality *filter*, not a guarantee: flawed
  papers get published, and good work sometimes dies on the floor.
- **Open access** — the publisher charges authors (or the authors' funders) instead
  of readers, so everyone can download the paper free.
- **Paywall** — the wall you hit when a journal charges for access. Legal ways
  around one live in [Chapter 1](01-anatomy-of-a-paper.md).
- **References / citations** — the numbered works a paper builds on. The backbone
  of a field; a few papers get cited by nearly everyone.
- **Supplementary materials** — the appendix with extra tables and details the main
  paper didn't have room for.
- **Trial registration** — publishing the study's plan (who, what, measures) in a
  public registry *before* the study runs. ClinicalTrials.gov IDs look like
  NCT12345678. Lets you check that no goalposts were moved later.

## People and setups

- **n** — the number of participants studied ("n = 312"). Bigger-in-the-right-people
  beats bigger-anyway.
- **Cohort** — a defined group of people followed over time (e.g., 40,000 nurses
  tracked for 15 years).
- **Inclusion / exclusion criteria** — the rules determining who got into a study.
  The rules are where a lot of quiet bias lives.
- **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)** — participants assigned by chance to
  treatment or comparison groups. The cleanest common design for separating cause
  from coincidence.
- **Double-blind** — neither participants (nor experimenters) knew who got the real
  treatment. Prevents the placebo wiggle and the measurement wiggle.
- **Placebo** — a pretend treatment that measures what expectation and attention do.
- **Standard of care** — the treatment doctors currently normally give; what a new
  treatment should beat.
- **Prospective / retrospective** — a study planned, then run forward; or a look
  backward through records. Prospective usually lands cleaner answers.
- **Cross-sectional / longitudinal** — a single-moment snapshot; vs. tracking the
  same people across time.

## Claims and evidence

- **Correlation vs. causation** — "these two move together" vs. "one makes the
  other happen." Watching shows correlation; randomly assigning people shows
  you the difference. (See [Chapter 3](03-strong-vs-weak-claims.md).)
- **Effect size** — how *big* the difference is. Small, significant differences
  exist and often don't matter to any single person.
- **Statistical significance** — the pattern would be unusual if nothing were going
  on (conventionally p < 0.05). It does not mean big or important.
- **p-value** — shorthand for the above. A measure of surprise, not of truth size.
- **Confidence interval (CI)** — the likely range for the true answer. The tighter,
  the surer; when the range spans "no effect at all," the result hasn't been
  separated from no effect.
- **Odds ratio / risk ratio / hazard ratio** — tidy ways of comparing "how often"
  between two groups; 1.0 is "no difference." Always convert back to absolute
  numbers for yourself.
- **Absolute vs. relative risk** — "risk doubled" (relative) vs. "1 in 10,000
  became 2 in 10,000" (absolute). Same figure, very different feel. Ask for
  absolute.
- **Primary / secondary outcome** — the planned star measure, vs. the bonus
  measures. Headlines love to promote a secondary to the top billing.
- **Publication bias** — positive results get published more easily than negative
  ones, so the visible literature leans successful. Absence of failures is
  information.
- **Replication / reproducibility** — another team finding the same answer
  (replication) or the same data producing the same answer (reproducibility).
  Both are how findings get promoted from "suggestive."
- **Meta-analysis** — a study of studies; pooling many papers' results to answer a
  question collectively. Quality depends on inclusion criteria and on how honestly
  it combined like-with-like.
- **Systematic review** — a paper that seeks out *all* the studies on a question by
  a stated recipe, then analyzes them. Often pairs with a meta-analysis.
- **Case study / case series** — reports about one or a few patients. Vital early
  signals; not proof of anything general.
- **Conflicts of interest (COI)** — situations where a study's authors or funders
  have a stake in the outcome. Disclosed isn't bad; undisclosed is.
- **Retraction** — a formally published paper withdrawn by a journal, usually for
  error or misconduct. Retraction Watch tracks these; retracted papers still float
  around the internet, so a careful reader checks.

## A closing word

You will meet these words repeatedly and they'll get boring in the best way — the
way a familiar street gets boring. That's the sign you're home.

> **Education only.** This glossary explains research vocabulary; it doesn't tell
> you what to do about your health, your money, or your legal questions. The right
> professionals remain exactly as important as ever.
